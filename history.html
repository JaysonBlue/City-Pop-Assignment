The History of City Pop

City Pop developed in Japan during a period of rapid cultural and economic change. While the genre became closely associated with the 1980s, its sound grew from musical experimentation that began during the previous decade.

SUGAR BABE 1970s musical roots

The Roots

1970s

City Pop did not appear as a fully formed genre in the 1980s. Its roots can be traced through the musical changes taking place in Japan during the 1970s. Japanese musicians were heavily influenced by Western styles, blending them with their own musical traditions to create a unique sound that would later be recognized as City Pop. An important part of this development was the growth of Japan's "New Music" scene in the 70's. Rather than simply reproducing American or European stylizing of music, Japanese artists began combining these outside influences with their own songwriting traditions. Sugar Babe, formed by Taeko Ohnuki and Tatsuro Yamashita, is often discussed as an early example of this transition. Their 1975 album "Songs" incorporated American pop and soul influences while maintaining a distinctly Japanese songwriting identity. The term "City Pop" was still taking shape during this period. By the late 1970s, Japanese record companies and music publications were beginning to use terms such as "city pops" to describe music with a sophisticated, modern, and urban atmosphere. At this point, the boundaries were loose, and the music had not yet become the clearly recognizable style that would emerge during the following decade.

Tatsuro Yamashita Ride on Time

The Rise

Early 1980s

By the beginning of the 1980s, the musical ideas that had been developing throughout the previous decade were becoming much more apparent in Japanese popular music. City Pop was increasingly associated with the experience of living in a modern Japanese city. Cars, nightlife, travel, romance, and the excitement of an increasingly affluent consumer culture became recurring subjects, giving the music an atmosphere that felt connected to contemporary urban life. The sound itself was becoming increasingly polished. Japanese musicians were working with talented studios, more sophisticated arrangements, and increasingly advanced recording technology. Jazz influenced harmony could sit alongside funk rhythms, smooth R&B grooves, electric guitars, synthesizers, and carefully layered vocals. Rather than fitting neatly into one musical category, City Pop became a meeting point for many different sounds. The genre also began reaching a much larger audience. Tatsuro Yamashita's 1980 single "Ride on Time" became a major success, while artists including Mariya Takeuchi, Eiichi Ohtaki, and others helped establish the sound within mainstream Japanese music. By this point, City Pop was no longer simply an emerging musical experiment. It was becoming part of the sound of contemporary Japan.

The Peak

Mid to Late 1980s

City Pop reached its greatest height during the 1980s. Its polished sound became closely connected with the image of Japan's rapidly developing cities. The music often presented an idealized version of modern life: driving through the city at night, spending an evening with someone special, traveling to the coast, or enjoying the comforts of an increasingly wealthy society. It was music that could make ordinary urban life feel glamorous. Musically, this was also where City Pop as a genre became especially distinctive. Its arrangements could draw from funk and R&B while incorporating jazz harmony, soft rock, disco, and electronic music. Bass lines and drum grooves provided a strong rhythmic foundation, while electric piano, synthesizers, guitar, horns, and layered backing vocals filled out the arrangements. The result was clean and detailed, with each instrument given a deliberate place within the mix. The quality of these productions was closely connected to the musicians who created them. Japan had a growing community of highly skilled session musicians and arrangers, many of whom could move comfortably between jazz, rock, pop, and funk. This allowed City Pop records to combine the accessibility of pop music with arrangements that rewarded closer listening. City Pop also became part of a larger visual culture. Album covers, fashion, advertisements, automobiles, beaches, skyscrapers, and neon lit streets all contributed to the genre's image of modern Japanese life. The music was helping create an aesthetic around what the city represented. This period produced many of the records that are now considered central to the City Pop sound. Artists such as Tatsuro Yamashita, Mariya Takeuchi, Anri, Junko Ohashi, Toshiki Kadomatsu, and Masayoshi Takanaka each approached the style differently, but their music shared an interest in sophisticated arrangements and the possibilities of modern studio production. City Pop had become more than a collection of musical influences; it had developed into a recognizable cultural aesthetic.

The Decline

1990s

The environment that had allowed City Pop to flourish began to change toward the end of the 1980s. Japan's economic bubble burst, bringing an end to the atmosphere of rapid growth and optimism that had surrounded much of the previous decade. At the same time, Japanese popular music was moving in new directions, with different styles and performers becoming increasingly prominent. City Pop did not simply disappear overnight. Many of its musicians continued making music, and elements of its sound continued to appear in Japanese pop and other genres. However, the particular combination of music, technology, consumer culture, and urban optimism that had defined its golden age was no longer at the center of Japanese popular music. By the 1990s, City Pop had largely become associated with the musical world of the previous decade. Its original era had come to an end, leaving behind a large catalog of music that would eventually be rediscovered by a completely different generation.
